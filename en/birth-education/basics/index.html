physiologic birthPhysiological birth

Physiological birth is birth powered by the innate capacities of mother and baby: labour starts on its own and progresses on its own, with body and baby working together, without drugs or procedures to drive it.

It doesn't mean you must give birth at home, or refuse all medical care. You can stay close to it in a hospital, a birth centre or at home, and accepting medical help when it's truly needed is also a choice. The point is to give the body the chance to work the way it was made to first.

I often say your body already knows how to give birth. Our job is not to disturb it, and to learn to listen to it.

Definition: American College of Nurse-Midwives and two other midwifery organisations, consensus statement "Supporting Healthy and Normal Physiologic Childbirth", 2012

the hormones of birthBirth hormones

In labour your body releases a set of hormones that work together to drive labour, ease pain and help you and your baby fall in love straight after birth. Your hormones affect your baby, and your baby's affect you.

Oxytocinthe hormone of love

Drives contractions and labour; after birth it helps the placenta come and reduces bleeding, and brings love and connection. It's shy: it flows most easily when you feel safe, quiet and loved.

Beta-endorphinsnatural pain relief

The body's own painkillers. They rise as labour intensifies, helping you into a focused, dreamy "labour land".

Adrenalinecatecholamines

Stress hormones. If you're frightened or disturbed in labour they rise, which can reduce oxytocin and slow labour; but a brief rise just before birth helps with the final push.

Prolactinthe mothering hormone

Prepares for breastfeeding. It rises late in labour and after birth, helping you settle into caring for your baby.

That's why birth needs quiet, dim light, warmth and no disturbance. It isn't fussiness; it's protecting your hormones.

Source: Sarah J. Buckley, Hormonal Physiology of Childbearing, Childbirth Connection, 2015 (PMC summary)

the stages of laborStages of labour

Labour runs from regular contractions until both baby and placenta are born. The World Health Organization (WHO) describes it like this:

  1. First stage: latent phaseContractions become regular and the cervix thins and opens to 5 cm. Its length varies a lot; with a first baby it can be long, and that's normal.This phase is usually best spent at home: eat, rest, walk.
  2. First stage: active phaseThe cervix opens from 5 cm to fully dilated (10 cm); contractions get stronger and closer.WHO: usually no longer than 12 hours for a first baby, and 10 hours for later babies.
  3. TransitionThe end of the active phase: the most intense part, when you may feel like giving up. Many women say "I can't do this", which often means the baby is nearly here.
  4. Second stageFrom full dilation to the birth of your baby, often with an urge to bear down.WHO: usually within 3 hours for a first baby, and 2 hours for later babies.
  5. Third stageFrom baby's birth to the birth of the placenta. Skin-to-skin and letting baby find the breast boost oxytocin and help the placenta come.

Source: WHO, Intrapartum care for a positive childbirth experience, 2018

questions parents ask meCommon questions

Do I really have to push?
In short: yes, but mostly your body does the pushing

In the second stage, many women feel a strong, unstoppable urge to bear down, a bit like needing to open your bowels. It's a reflex. You don't need someone counting "one, two, three, hold your breath and push!"

WHO (2018) recommends supporting women to follow their own urge to push. A Cochrane review comparing spontaneous pushing with directed, breath-holding pushing found no clear difference in the length of the second stage, perineal tears or rates of vaginal birth, and suggests women push in the way that feels right to them.

  • Choose a position that feels good: upright, kneeling, all fours or side-lying. Gravity helps
  • Making sound is fine; low tones help the pelvic floor relax
  • With an epidural, if you can't feel the urge, you can discuss waiting a little before pushing
In class we practise listening to the body and labour positions, so you can recognise how your body wants to push.

Sources: WHO 2018 intrapartum care recommendations; Lemos et al., Cochrane CD009124, 2017

What are the benefits of delayed cord clamping?
In short: your baby gets back more of their own blood, and more iron

At birth, some of your baby's blood is still in the cord and placenta. Waiting before clamping lets that blood keep flowing back to your baby.

  • WHO recommends waiting at least 1 minute after birth before clamping the cord
  • Haemoglobin and iron: a Cochrane review (2013) found that full-term babies with delayed clamping had higher haemoglobin after birth; at 3 to 6 months, babies with early clamping were about 2.65 times as likely to be iron deficient
  • Birth weight was also slightly higher
  • To be aware of: slightly more delayed-clamping babies needed phototherapy for jaundice, so the authors concluded delayed clamping is likely beneficial as long as jaundice care is available

If your baby needs immediate resuscitation, the team will decide based on the situation. You can write "delayed cord clamping" in your birth plan and discuss it with your doctor beforehand.

Those one or two minutes, with your baby on your chest and the cord still connecting you, are the last moments of being one body.

Sources: WHO 2018; McDonald et al., Cochrane CD004074, 2013

Can I avoid an episiotomy?
In short: you can discuss it with your doctor; routine episiotomy is no longer recommended

An episiotomy is a cut to the tissue between the vagina and anus as the baby is born. It used to be routine in many places, but the recommendations have changed.

  • WHO 2018: routine or liberal episiotomy is not recommended for spontaneous vaginal birth
  • Cochrane review (2017): compared with routine use, using episiotomy only when needed may mean about 30% fewer severe perineal tears (low-certainty evidence), with little or no difference in blood loss, infection or pain-relief use at 10 days
  • When it may still be needed: for example if the baby needs to be born quickly, or with forceps or vacuum birth

What you can do:

  • Ask your doctor directly: "Do you usually do episiotomies? In what situations?"
  • Write "no routine episiotomy" in your birth plan
  • Push slowly in the second stage and choose upright or side-lying positions so the perineum has time to stretch
  • Research supports warm compresses on the perineum during the second stage, and antenatal perineal massage from late pregnancy for first-time mothers, to reduce perineal injury (discuss with your care team)
You have the right to know and the right to ask. Asking isn't distrust; it's deciding together about your body.

Sources: WHO 2018; Jiang et al., Cochrane CD000081, 2017; Aasheim et al., Cochrane CD006672 (warm compresses), 2017; Beckmann & Stock, Cochrane CD005123 (antenatal perineal massage), 2013

When should I go to the hospital?
In short: follow your hospital's guidance, and call straight away for certain signs

Early labour with a first baby is often long. Arriving too early can mean being sent home, or starting procedures you may not need. Many women stay home until contractions are regular and strong.

  • A common guide: for a first baby, contractions about every 5 minutes, each lasting about 1 minute, for about an hour. Hospitals differ, so follow yours
  • Call the hospital straight away if your waters break (especially if the fluid is green, brown or smells), you have heavy bleeding, the baby moves much less, you have constant severe abdominal pain, fever, or headache with blurred vision
  • Ask at a check-up: "When should I call, and when should I just come in?"
While labouring at home, eat a little, take a warm shower, walk around, and let oxytocin build. We can plan when to leave together in class.

Sources: WHO 2018 intrapartum care recommendations; Health Promotion Administration maternal care site (Chinese)

Can I eat and drink in labour?
In short: if you are low-risk, yes

Many hospitals used to ban food in labour, worried about inhaling stomach contents if general anaesthesia were needed. Caesareans today mostly use spinal or epidural anaesthesia, so that risk is now very low.

  • WHO 2018: for low-risk women, eating and drinking during labour is recommended
  • Cochrane review (2013): for low-risk women, there is no evidence to justify restricting food and fluids in labour
  • Labour takes a lot of energy, like running a marathon. Small, easy-to-digest amounts work best: soup, congee, bananas, sports drinks, honey water

If you are high-risk, or your doctor thinks surgery may be needed, follow the hospital's advice. You can write "I'd like to eat and drink in labour" in your birth plan.

Sources: WHO 2018; Singata et al., Cochrane CD003930 (restricting oral intake in labour), 2013

Do I have to give birth lying down?
In short: no, you can choose the position that feels good

Lying on a bed is mostly convenient for staff to observe and assist; it isn't the best position for birth itself. Moving freely and staying upright lets gravity help and gives the pelvis more room to open.

  • WHO 2018: low-risk women are encouraged to move and stay upright in labour; in the second stage (without an epidural), women are encouraged to choose their own birth position, including upright ones
  • Cochrane review (2017): for women without an epidural, upright positions in the second stage meant a slightly shorter second stage and possibly fewer episiotomies and assisted births, but possibly slightly more second-degree tears and blood loss over 500 ml (low-certainty evidence)
  • Standing, kneeling, squatting, all fours, side-lying, sitting on a birth ball: try them all
Your body will tell you how it wants to move. In class we use BellydanceBirth movement and labour positions so your body already knows these feelings.

Sources: WHO 2018; Gupta et al., Cochrane CD002006, 2017

What does an epidural do, and do I need one?
In short: it relieves pain very well, but often brings other interventions with it; I suggest trying body-based methods and support first

An epidural is very effective pain relief, and that's true. But it doesn't only take the pain away; once it's in, a lot of other things in the birth room tend to change.

  • One intervention often leads to the next: you usually stay in bed with continuous fetal monitoring and an IV drip, and may need a urinary catheter. If labour slows, synthetic oxytocin is often added. A Cochrane review (2018) found women with epidurals had longer labours and needed oxytocin more often. This chain reaction is called the "cascade of intervention"
  • Your own hormones may drop: research summarised by Buckley (2015) suggests oxytocin and endorphins (the body's natural pain relief) may fall after an epidural, making synthetic oxytocin more likely to be needed
  • Assisted birth: a higher chance of forceps or vacuum, though in studies since 2005 using modern low-dose epidurals the difference is no longer clear
  • Common effects: low blood pressure, numb or weak legs, difficulty passing urine, and fever. When the mother has a fever, the baby is more likely to have blood tests or even antibiotics after birth (one 1997 study: fever in 14.5% vs 1.0%)
  • It doesn't always work: about 10–20% of women get incomplete pain relief and need it adjusted or redone
  • Rare but worth knowing: if the dura is accidentally punctured, a severe headache can follow, and for a few women headache and back pain last over a year
  • Breastfeeding: findings are mixed. In a review of 23 studies, 12 found a negative effect and 10 found none
  • Caesarean: randomised trials have not found that epidurals increase caesareans

To be honest about the other side: when labour is very long, you're exhausted, or there's a medical reason, an epidural can be a real help. A Scottish study of over 560,000 women (2024) found fewer serious complications among women who had an epidural, most clearly in those with a medical reason for it.

About birth trauma: research so far hasn't found that the epidural itself causes trauma. What tends to hurt women is losing their sense of control, not being treated well, and unexpected emergencies. Expecting an epidural to take all the pain away, and then having it not work well, can also be very hard.

I want you to discover first how much strength your body has. In our classes we practise breathing, movement and massage, and learn other natural pain-relief techniques, all ways to make the pain bearable. I see an epidural as an option for when it's truly needed, not the first option.

And I also want you to know: running away is nothing to be ashamed of. If, after truly understanding what an epidural can bring, you still choose one, that's okay. It's a decision you made with your eyes open. I'll still walk with you all the way through. Whatever form your birth takes, what matters most is giving birth with the intention of love.

Sources: Anim-Somuah et al., Cochrane CD000331, 2018; Buckley, Hormonal Physiology of Childbearing, 2015; Lieberman et al., Pediatrics, 1997; French et al., J Hum Lact, 2016; ESAIC guideline on the failing labour epidural, 2025; Kearns et al., BMJ, 2024; Jones et al., Cochrane overview of labour pain management, 2012

Does having a doula really help?
In short: research supports it; continuous support helps

A doula doesn't do medical procedures. She stays with you from start to finish, offering emotional support, comfort measures and information, and helping you, your partner and your care team communicate.

  • Cochrane review (2017, 27 trials, nearly 16,000 women): women with continuous support in labour were more likely to have a spontaneous vaginal birth, had shorter labours, fewer caesareans, assisted births and pain medication, and fewer negative feelings about their birth
  • The effect was clearest when the support person was not a hospital staff member, but someone there just for you
  • WHO (2018) also recommends that women have a companion of their choice during labour

Read more in my article Why have a doula?

Sources: Bohren et al., Cochrane CD003766, 2017; WHO 2018

Can I hold my baby right after birth?
In short: yes, skin-to-skin is good for you both

Right after birth, your baby lies directly on your bare chest, covered with a blanket. This is skin-to-skin contact. Many babies slowly crawl up and find the breast on their own.

  • Cochrane review (2016, 46 studies, nearly 4,000 mothers and babies): early skin-to-skin contact supports breastfeeding; even when it's brief, more mothers are still breastfeeding at 1 to 4 months
  • Skin-to-skin also keeps baby warm and settled, and raises your oxytocin, helping the placenta come and reducing bleeding
  • After a caesarean, you can also ask about skin-to-skin in theatre or recovery
  • You can write "immediate skin-to-skin, routine checks delayed" in your birth plan
This is the first time you and your baby truly meet. For that first hour, try not to be disturbed.

Source: Moore et al., Cochrane CD003519, 2016
